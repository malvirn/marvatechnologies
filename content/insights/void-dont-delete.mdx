## TL;DR

When a financial record was wrong, do not delete it. Mark it voided, record who did it and why, and post a reversing entry. Do the same for people and customers: archive them rather than erase them.

## The real scenario

A receipt was entered against the wrong invoice. The tempting fix is to delete the row. But that payment may already have changed the invoice balance, appeared on a customer statement and been posted to the ledger.

Deleting it makes the receipt disappear while everything it touched stays changed.

## Why it matters

An accounting system has to explain itself. A year later someone will ask what happened to a payment, and "the row is gone" is not an answer. Voiding keeps the story: what was recorded, who reversed it, when and why.

## When to use it (and when not to)

Use voiding for anything that affects money, stock or legal records. Hard deletes are fine for genuinely disposable data, such as a draft that was never submitted or a temporary token.

## How it works in Centric

**Extra columns, not missing rows.** A voidable record gets fields for the void instead of being removed:

```sql
alter table public.invoice_payments
  add column if not exists voided_at timestamptz,
  add column if not exists voided_by uuid references auth.users(id),
  add column if not exists void_reason text,
  add column if not exists reversal_journal_entry_id uuid references public.journal_entries(id);
```

**One function does the whole void.** It checks the caller is at accounting level, insists on a reason, and locks the payment and the invoice row while it works so nothing changes underneath it:

```sql
select * into payment_row from public.invoice_payments
where id = p_invoice_payment_id
for update;

if payment_row.voided_at is not null then
  raise exception 'This receipt has already been voided';
end if;
```

It also refuses to void a payment that created overpayment credit until that credit has been resolved, so one correction cannot silently break another record.

**Everything else must ignore voided rows.** This is the part people forget. Sums, statements and balances filter on `voided_at is null`:

```sql
select coalesce(sum(amount), 0)
from public.repair_job_payments
where repair_job_id = p_target_id and voided_at is null;
```

**People are archived, not erased.** Customers carry a `deleted_at` timestamp and can be restored from an archived list, and their jobs and account entries are kept.

## Common mistakes

- Deleting a row that other records depend on.
- Voiding the record but forgetting to reverse its ledger entry.
- Not filtering voided rows out of totals and statements.
- Allowing anyone to void. It needs a role check and a reason.
- Allowing a double void.

## Practical checklist

<Checklist>
<li>Money records are never hard-deleted.</li>
<li>A void stores who, when and why, and links to its reversal.</li>
<li>Voiding is one transaction with the affected rows locked.</li>
<li>Every total and statement excludes voided rows.</li>
<li>Voiding is limited by role and needs a written reason.</li>
<li>Customers and users are archived, with a way to restore them.</li>
</Checklist>

## From my builds

In Centric, voiding exists for invoice payment receipts and related customer credit refunds, with later migrations updating statements and sync logic to ignore voided payments. Each time voiding was added to a new record type, the totals and statements had to be changed too. That is the cost of this pattern, and it is what keeps the history trustworthy.
